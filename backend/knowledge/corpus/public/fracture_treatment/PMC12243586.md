# Brachial plexus nerve block versus haematoma block for closed reduction of distal radius fracture in adults: The BLOCK Trial – a protocol for a multicentre randomised controlled trial

## 摘要

AbstractIntroductionDistal radius fractures account for one-fifth of all fractures in the active elderly population and may cause chronic pain, loss of hand function and reduced work productivity, imposing a significant socioeconomic burden. Most are initially treated with closed reduction and casting, but 30% subsequently require surgery due to insufficient realignment. The current approaches for analgesia for closed reduction are suboptimal. A brachial plexus nerve block provides complete pain relief and muscle relaxation distal to the elbow, potentially creating better conditions for realignment of the fractured bone ends. This may ultimately translate into reduced need for surgery and result in better functional outcomes and fewer complications compared to a haematoma block, which is the current standard care in Denmark.Methods and analysisThe BLOCK Trial is an investigator-initiated, parallel-group, allocation-concealed, outcome assessor and analyst-blinded, superiority, randomised, controlled, clinical multicentre trial performed at 11 Danish emergency departments. Eligible adult patients with a distal radius fracture who need closed reduction will be included and allocated 1:1 to either an ultrasound-guided brachial plexus nerve block or a haematoma block. The primary outcome is the proportion of patients with distal radius fracture surgery 90 days after closed reduction. We will include 1716 participants to detect or discard a relative risk reduction of surgery of 20%. Secondary outcomes include treatment-related complications, patient-reported wrist function, pain during closed reduction and proportion of patients with unacceptable radiographic fracture position immediately after closed reduction.Ethics and disseminationfThe trial is approved by the Danish Medicines Agency and the Danish Research Ethics Committees (EU CT number: 2024-512191-35-00). All results will be summarised on www.theblocktrial.com, clinicaltrials.gov and euclinicaltrials.eu after publication. Primary and secondary outcome results from 0 to 90 days will be presented in the main article and submitted to a peer-reviewed journal. Results from outcomes on the 12-month follow-up will be presented separately.Trial registration numberNCT06678438.

## Introduction

### Anaesthesia for distal radius fracture

Distal radius fractures account for one-fifth of all fractures in the active elderly population, and numbers are increasing due to a growing elderly population.1 2 Following a distal radius fracture, a substantial proportion of patients do not regain prior hand function, which is the foremost concern for most patients.3 Other consequences may be chronic pain and reduced work productivity, resulting in a significant socioeconomic burden.4 5 Most distal radius fractures are displaced and treated with closed reduction, where the doctor applies pressure and traction to manually realign the bone endings before fixating the fracture in a cast.6 However, in approximately 30% of the cases, surgery is needed because of insufficient realignment or fracture instability.2

Optimal analgesia is essential for a comfortable closed reduction of the fracture and may be crucial for the quality of the reduction and a better outcome.7 Despite striving for adequate analgesia, the repositioning procedure is often still painful, and current evidence is insufficient to conclude on the best analgesia method.810 In Denmark, a haematoma block is the standard analgesia method. In this procedure, local anaesthetic is injected into the haematoma surrounding the fractured bone endings. The injection is often painful in itself and suboptimal for pain relief during the closed reduction.11 Using intravenous regional anaesthesia (IVRA) a local anaesthetic is injected into the venous system of the arm and isolated from the central circulation by a tourniquet. In a Cochrane systematic review, all five trials comparing IVRA to a haematoma block found that IVRA provided better analgesia for closed reduction.8 Four of the trials found that IVRA provided easier fracture reduction, and three found better anatomical postreduction bone alignment compared with using a haematoma block. IVRA is therefore the recommended first choice anaesthetic method for closed reduction of distal radius fractures in the UK.12 However, IVRA is time-consuming and has a higher risk of complications compared with haematoma block, which may be why it is not more widely implemented.8

An ultrasound-guided brachial plexus block is a standard anaesthetic method for surgery of the antebrachium and hand. A brachial plexus block may be superior to both IVRA and haematoma block as analgesia for closed reduction because it provides muscle relaxation distal to the elbow in addition to complete pain relief.13 When performed by an experienced anaesthetist, it is a quick, safe and low-cost procedure.14 15

A large proportion of patients undergo surgery despite attempts of closed reduction. Consensus on surgical indication seems lacking. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) advises against surgery for patients aged ≥65 years but acknowledges the limitations of using age as a proxy for physical function.16 Danish guidelines are inconsistent, and recent studies support more liberal approaches.1719 Surgery increases the risk of complications such as infection, tendon rupture, complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) and mechanical symptoms.20 Furthermore, avoiding surgery reduces costs and spares the patient from trauma, perioperative stress and potential adverse events (AEs) of opioid use and anaesthesia.5 Therefore, avoiding fracture surgery after a closed reduction is highly important to patients, clinically relevant and cost-saving.

A recently published systematic review included all randomised clinical trials evaluating nerve blocks for pain management for closed reduction of distal radius fractures.21 Six smaller trials exist, and of those, only two evaluated bone realignment. Just one trial (n=50) reported on patients undergoing surgery after closed reduction and evaluated the wrist function. National and international recommendations are inconsistent, outdated or lacking information on the best analgesic method for closed reduction—none mention nerve blocks as an option.12 16

### Aim

To evaluate the beneficial and harmful effects of an ultrasound-guided brachial plexus nerve block for patients with a distal radius fracture managed with closed reduction, compared with a haematoma block.

### Hypotheses

We hypothesise that using a brachial plexus nerve block for closed reduction of a distal radius fracture can reduce the need for subsequent surgery compared with using a haematoma block. This is based on the hypothesis that the brachial plexus nerve block provides muscle relaxation and complete pain relief during the closed reduction, allowing for better anatomical alignment of the fractured bone ends. Additionally, we hypothesise that a brachial plexus nerve block for closed reduction will lead to better functional outcomes and fewer complications than a haematoma block.

## Methods and analysis

### Trial design

The BLOCK Trial (www.theblocktrial.com) is an investigator-initiated, multicentre, parallel-group, allocation-concealed, outcome assessor and analyst-blinded, superiority, randomised, controlled, clinical trial with a 1:1 allocation. Participants’ timeline is depicted in figure 1. The trial protocol has been written according to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials and the trial will take place in at least eleven emergency departments (appendix 1) from four regions in Denmark.22

### Inclusion criteria

- Adults (age≥18 years) with a distal radius fracture in need of closed reduction.

### Exclusion criteria

- Patients who would never qualify for surgery according to local guidelines.
- Lack of informed consent.
- No Danish Central Person Register (CPR) number.
- Allergies to the trial medication.
- Distal radius fracture initially deemed to require surgery regardless of the outcome of the closed reduction.
- Open fracture (fracture-related wound requiring sutures).
- Other fractures on the same extremity (NOT including distal ulna fracture(s)).
- Bilateral distal radius fractures both requiring closed reduction.
- Concomitant medical or surgical condition taking priority over the closed reduction of the distal radius fracture.

### Recruitment

Patients diagnosed with a distal radius fracture in need of closed reduction will be screened for eligibility by emergency physicians according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Screening may be limited to certain time slots due to a lack of 24-hour anaesthesiologic expertise at all sites. Eligible patients will be informed about the trial verbally and in writing, so they fully understand all aspects of the trial. Since urgent closed reduction is necessary for pain relief and to minimise nerve, vessel and soft tissue damage, patients will be given up to 30 min to consider participation and give their written informed consent (online supplemental material). The enrolment will continue until the sample size has been obtained.

### Allocation

The participants will be randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention or the control group. The allocation table consists of block sizes of six and is generated by an external provider (Region Hovedstadens Apotek). The same external provider is also responsible for creating sealed, opaque envelopes containing the randomisation number and the allocated analgesic method.

### Blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention, it is unfeasible to blind the participants, the doctor performing the brachial plexus nerve block and the doctor performing the haematoma block and the closed reduction. As a result, data on patient-reported outcome measures will be reported unblinded. Importantly, other caregivers and all outcome assessors are blinded, including the doctor evaluating the radiographic results and assessing the primary outcome, the need for surgery and all personnel involved in the subsequent outcome evaluations. Two independent statisticians will perform blinded analyses on all outcomes, and blinded conclusions will be written and agreed on before unblinding. Unblinding of a participant during the trial will only be allowed due to safety and can be done in the electronic case report form (eCRF).

### Intervention

The participants in this trial will be equally and randomly allocated to one of two groups before the closed reduction:

- Intervention group: participants will receive an ultrasound-guided brachial plexus nerve block, performed by a doctor with appropriate procedural expertise. A total of 30 mL of lidocaine (10 mg/mL) with epinephrine (5 µg/mL) is the recommended volume to be administered as predefined multiple injections. The brachial plexus nerve block is performed ultrasound-guided either with a lateral infraclavicular approach or an axillary approach, depending on the patient’s anatomy and the preference of the performing doctor (see appendix 2).
- Control group: participants will receive no peripheral nerve block but a haematoma block, with or without supplemental sedation and/or systemic analgesia, which is standard care in Denmark.

Before randomisation, all participants are allowed to receive conventional (oral or intravenous) analgesics according to usual clinical practice. After the allocated anaesthesia has been performed, both groups will receive identical treatments according to local guidelines.

### Evaluation of block success

The brachial plexus nerve block is tested sensorily and motorically according to the anatomical relationship between dermatomes and myotomes and common anatomical variations.23

The sensory block is tested according to generally accepted principles by placing a cold glass ampoule or wiping an alcohol swab on the participant’s skin in the following areas:

- Musculocutaneous nerve: the lateral forearm midway between the wrist and the elbow.
- Radial nerve: dorsal side of the second finger, corresponding to the proximal phalanx.
- Median nerve: palmar side of the second finger corresponding to the distal phalanx.
- Ulnar nerve: palmar side of the fifth finger, corresponding to the distal phalanx.

The motor block is tested by examining the motor function of the following nerves:

- Musculocutaneous nerve: flexion of the elbow.
- Radial nerve: extension of the wrist.
- Median nerve: opposition of the first finger.
- Ulnar nerve: finger abduction/adduction.

The block quality is evaluated according to international standards.24 The sensory block is quantified on a three-point scale: 1 point=normal cold sensation; 2 points=reduced cold sensation; 3 points=no cold sensation.25 The motor block is evaluated compared with the contralateral arm on a four-point Manual Muscle Testing scale: 0=no difference, flexion against force possible; 1=slight difference, flexion against slight force hardly possible; 2=significant difference, flexion hardly possible; 3=paralysis.25

The block is defined as successful if the participant has no cold sensation (scores 3) for all four peripheral nerves, and the participant has a significant difference or paralysis (scores 2–3 points) for all four peripheral nerves.

### In case of unsuccessful block

If the block is unsuccessful after 30 min, the doctors involved in the treatment decide the best course of action for the participant. This could include one of the following: (1) No further analgesia is needed—closed reduction will be performed without supplemental analgesia. (2) Supplementary nerve block(s) within the proposed upper limit of lidocaine with epinephrine (7 mg lidocaine/kg body weight) is performed. (3) Supplementary systemic analgesia and/or sedative before the closed reduction. (4) Other treatment according to standard care including a haematoma block.

### Training and certification

To ensure a high level of uniform performance, doctors with expertise in regional anaesthesia and the procedure will receive visual, written and in-person instructions on how to perform the nerve block.

### Outcomes

#### Primary outcome

- Proportion of patients with distal radius fracture surgery 90 days after closed reduction.

#### Secondary outcomes

- Composite outcome of treatment-related complications: locoregional neurological complications from affected arm, local anaesthetic systemic toxicity, soft tissue injuries (including skin injuries) related to the closed reduction procedure, infection at the site of needle puncture, vessel puncture requiring subsequent treatment (including compression), pneumothorax, allergic reaction, new bone or joint injury (eg, dislocation or fracture) after the closed reduction and pulmonary embolism within 90 days after closed reduction.
- Patient-Rated Wrist Evaluation (PRWE) 90 days after closed reduction.
- Maximum pain score (Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) 0–10) in the affected wrist during closed reduction(s).
- Proportion of patients with unacceptable radiographic fracture position immediately after closed reduction, according to the AAOS guidelines.16

#### Exploratory outcomes

- Pain at rest (NRS 0–10) in the affected wrist immediately, 90 days and 12 months after closed reduction.
- Maximum pain (NRS 0–10) in the affected wrist within the first 24 hours after closed reduction.
- Proportion of patients with CRPS 90 days and 12 months after closed reduction.
- Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) Score 90 days and 12 months after closed reduction.
- PRWE 12 months after closed reduction.
- European Quality of Life 5 Dimension 5 Level (EQ-5D-5L) score 90 days and 12 months after closed reduction.
- Number of attempts used for closed reduction.
- Difficulty of closed reduction of the fracture: the doctor reported ease (NRS 0–10; 0=very easy, 10=very difficult).
- Discomfort related to the anaesthetic procedure (NRS 0–10; 0=no discomfort, 10=very uncomfortable).
- Overall satisfaction with the treatment 0–24 hours after initiation of the anaesthesia (NRS 0–10; 0=very unsatisfied, 10=very satisfied).
- Proportion of patients with unacceptable radiographic position, according to the AAOS guidelines at first follow-up in the orthopaedic outpatient clinic (typically 10–14 days post reduction).16
- Proportion of individual complications in the composite secondary outcome.
- Proportion of patients having one or more serious adverse events (SAEs), including events related to loss of hand function, within 90 days after closed reduction.
- Proportion of patients having one or more AEs within 24 hours after closed reduction.

The outcome collection timeframe is displayed in figure 2. The definition of outcomes is described in appendix 3.

### Data collection and data management

All trial data will be collected in the participants’ eCRF via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap)—a secure web application for managing online databases and surveys.26 Demographic data and data regarding the haematoma block, intervention and closed reduction will be assessed and collected by clinical personnel trained in the database. Follow-up data on patient-reported outcome measures will be collected via electronic questionnaires automatically entered in the eCRF. Remaining follow-up data will be assessed and collected by trained investigators in a Central Coordinating Centre established to improve data monitoring and quality, and to limit the amount of data for clinicians to enter, securing uniform and high-quality data. They are blinded to the participants’ allocation and all outcomes not directly collected by themselves. Data will be managed according to the Danish Data Protection Act, General Data Protection Regulation, Danish national law and Good Clinical Practice (GCP).

### Statistics

Full details on statistical considerations and a comprehensive statistical analysis plan, including pre-specified subgroup analyses, will be published before enrolling the final participant.

#### Sample size

Approximately 30% of patients with a distal radius fracture undergoing current standard treatment will experience the primary outcome.8 We consider a relative risk reduction of 20% clinically relevant, correlating to an absolute risk reduction of 6% from 30% to 24%. With an alpha of 5% and a power of 80%, 1716 (2×858) participants are needed to detect or discard the superiority of the intervention in reducing the proportion of patients having surgery.

#### Statistical methods for the primary outcome

The primary outcome analysis will be based on the intention-to-treat population. The primary outcome will be compared between the two groups using a generalised linear model with log link and adjusted for site. Risks are reported as relative risks with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Secondary analyses will be performed on (1) a modified intention-to-treat analysis including the intention-to-treat population excluding participants with major protocol violations.27 28 (2) a per-protocol analysis on all participants randomised and receiving intended treatment but excluding participants experiencing unsuccessful plexus brachialis block as defined in the protocol (see ‘Evaluation of block success’). Missing data will be addressed as suggested by Jakobsen et al.29

### Monitoring

The trial will be internally and externally monitored. The Central Coordinating Centre will conduct internal monitoring on blinded data, including monitoring of trial recruitment, assessment of quality and timeliness of data entry. The Danish GCP-unit will externally monitor the trial to oversee and ensure the quality, accuracy and adherence to the trial protocol. In addition, an independent data monitoring and safety committee (DMSC) will be established to monitor the safety of the trial.

An interim analysis will be performed when data on the primary outcome have been reported for 858 (50%) participants. The DMSC will receive data to analyse the primary outcome and SAEs and may request additional data if needed. LanDeMets group sequential monitoring boundaries and O’Brien Fleming alpha-spending function will be applied, and the DMSC will provide recommendations to the steering committee about stopping or continuing the trial. The DMSC may suggest if (and when) an additional interim analysis should be conducted. The trial may be terminated prematurely based on recommendations from the DMSC for three reasons: (1) futility, (2) efficacy or (3) harm. A separate, detailed DMSC charter will be published on the trial's official webpage (www.theblocktrial.com).

### Safety

Both anaesthetic methods are safe procedures with a low risk of adverse reactions (ARs) and complications when performed by experienced personnel.14 Like any other procedure using a needle, there is a risk of soreness, erythema and minor haematoma at the site of the needle insertion. Possible AEs investigated and reported in the literature regarding brachial plexus block include vessel puncture, pneumothorax and locoregional neurological complications.14 30 However, these events are very rare and typically easily treated and transient. Lidocaine with epinephrine is used in both groups. It is a local anaesthetic approved for infiltration analgesia and peripheral nerve blocks. It will be administered in safe doses at a maximum of 7 mg/kg, translating to 350 mg (35 mL) for a 50 kg patient, to avoid the risk of systemic toxicity.31 AEs, ARs, serious adverse reactions (SARs) and suspected unexpected serious adverse reactions will be recorded from the performance of the analgesic procedure (plexus brachialis block or haematoma block) until 24 hours after closed reduction. SAEs will be recorded up to 90 days after closed reduction. Surgical reduction of the distal radius fracture, including related admission to hospital, will not be categorised as an SAE. The sponsor is responsible for continuing monitoring of the harm–benefit ratio and will submit an annual safety report to Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS), including SARs and an assessment of participant safety.

### Participant withdrawal

Participation is voluntary and the participant can withdraw from the trial at any time without reason.

### Patient and public involvement

A qualitative survey has been conducted in advance to understand what is important to patients with a distal radius fracture.10 Two of the major themes were pain management and concerns about future functional abilities. These patient reflections are incorporated as substantial elements of the trial outcomes. Patients have also been commenting on the wording and relevance of the follow-up questions designed by the investigators. The PRWE, DASH and EQ-5D-5L are validated questionnaires to detect patient important aspects regarding daily function and quality of life. Furthermore, the study has been endorsed by the patient association the Danish Osteoporosis Association.

## Ethics and dissemination

A comprehensive protocol version with full details on the trial is available at euclinicaltrials.eu (EU CT number: 2024-512191-35-00). The BLOCK Trial will be carried out in accordance with this protocol, current EU regulations on clinical trials on medicinal products for human use and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and GCP. The trial is approved by the Danish Medicines Agency and the Danish Research Ethics Committees (EU CT number: 2024-512191-35-00) and the Danish Data Protection Agency (p-2024-16963).

The trial may improve treatment for patients with a distal radius fracture by comparing two commonly used procedures with low risk of complications.14 30

The interventions are evaluated based on patient-relevant outcomes, including the proportion of participants requiring subsequent wrist surgery. Avoiding surgery is intuitively beneficial in decreasing risks, complications and cost per patient.5 20

The correlation between radiographic bone alignment and patient-reported function in patients aged≥60 years appears limited.32 Therefore, in addition to radiology, the trial addresses pain management and patient-reported functional ability, which are the foremost concerns for patients with distal radius fractures.3 10 Closed reduction must be preceded by some form of anaesthesia. The expected risks and disadvantages for the participants are few and proportional to potential benefits.

The trial was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06678438) before enrolling the first participant. All trial material relevant to participants and providers is accessible on the trial website: www.theblocktrial.com, which will be continuously be updated according to trial progression. A summary of all trial results (positive, neutral and negative) will be published on www.theblocktrial.com, clinicaltrials.gov and euclinicaltrials.eu for broader access. Results from the primary and secondary outcomes from 0 to 90 days will be presented in the main article. Results from outcomes on 12-month follow-up will be published separately. Full details on reporting of the outcomes will be included in the statistical analysis plan published before enrolling the final participant. The manuscripts follow the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT).33 After the publication of results, access to data can be granted on reasonable request.

## Discussion

Current approaches for analgesia for closed reduction of distal radius fractures are suboptimal. This large-scale randomised controlled trial aims to investigate the effect of using a brachial plexus nerve block for patients with a distal radius fracture. It is powered to investigate both short-term and long-term patient-relevant effects compared with a haematoma block, which is the standard care in Denmark. It is conducted in partnership with the Collaboration for Evidence-based Practice and Research in Anaesthesia (CEPRA),34 and is a multidisciplinary effort between anaesthesiology, orthopaedic surgery and emergency medicine. The trial design follows international standards to minimise the risk of bias. However, as described in the ‘Blinding’ section, the nature of the intervention precludes participant blinding, which may increase the risk of performance bias and bias on patient-reported outcome measures. Importantly, all outcome assessors, including the doctors determining the need for surgery, are blinded, thereby minimising the risk of bias on the primary outcome: the proportion of patients needing surgery. The trial is designed as a large-scale, multicentre, pragmatic trial to enhance external validity and ensure that findings will be relevant and implementable in emergency department care. The primary outcome regarding fracture surgery is highly important to patients, clinically relevant, potentially cost-saving, valid and reliable. Patients not qualified for surgery according to local guidelines (eg, patients with low functional level) and patients with a fracture requiring surgery regardless of the closed reduction (eg, volar angulation) are excluded from this trial, to some extent limiting the generalisability of results. The exclusion criteria are based on local guidelines as no clear national or international consensus currently exists regarding surgical indications for this patient population, especially in the elderly or frail subgroups. The AAOS advises against surgery for patients aged 65 and older, using age as a surrogate for functional demand. However, the AAOS acknowledges the limitations of this approach and recommends that decision on surgery prioritise functional demands over age alone.16 Accordingly, a recent national Danish recommendation advocates for a nonsurgical approach for treating distal radius fractures in patients aged 65 years and older and suggests that surgical indication should be based on factors beyond radiological misalignment, such as biological age and functional level.17 A recent trial suggests more liberal approaches.18 New recommendations and evolving evidence may influence the number and characteristics of potentially eligible participants for this trial. The findings of this trial are expected to inform patients, clinicians and decision-makers alike, potentially optimising the treatment of displaced distal radius fractures in adults.

## Trial status and timeline

The first participant was enrolled December 2024. Enrolment is expected complete within Q4 2026. Trial status is updated continuously on www.theblocktrial.com.
